## Executive Function Model (Brown, 2008/2017)

Thomas Brown's model describes six clusters of executive functions impaired in ADHD:

| Cluster | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Activation | Organizing, prioritizing, and activating to work |
| Focus | Focusing, sustaining, and shifting attention to tasks |
| Effort | Regulating alertness, sustaining effort, processing speed |
| Emotion | Managing frustration and modulating emotions |
| Memory | Utilizing working memory and accessing recall |
| Action | Monitoring and self-regulating action |

## The Shame Cycle (Scott / Cook)

Six-stage cycle originally created by Danielle Joy Scott, LMFT; illustrated by Dr. Blair Cook. Central framework for understanding how shame compounds ADHD struggles.

### Stages

1. **Feeling Motivated** - Initial burst of energy and optimism about a task or goal
2. **Attempting to Get Things Done** - Stepping into action on the task
3. **Struggling to Stay on Task** - Focus drifts, executive function barriers emerge
4. **Feeling Like a Failure** - Internalized negative self-talk, often rooted in past criticism
5. **Seeking Instant Gratification** - Reaching for comfort or stimulation (scrolling, snacking, games)
6. **End-of-Day Regret** - Shame about how time was spent, which feeds back into Stage 1

### Hacks by Stage

**Stage 1 - Motivated:**
- Get realistic about expectations (Is this manageable? Enough time? Enough energy? Good environment?)
- Harness motivation into a plan - commit to a schedule, give yourself structure to refer back to

**Stage 2 - Attempting:**
- Break large tasks into smaller subtasks
- Use Goblin.tools for AI-assisted task decomposition

**Stage 3 - Struggling:**
- HALT check-in: Hungry? Angry? Lonely? Tired?
- Non-judgmental self-check: "How am I feeling right now?" "Where are my thoughts going?"
- Have a plan for this: switch to smaller tasks, or take a structured break (10-min walk, snack)

**Stage 4 - Feeling like a failure:**
- Identify the source: "Who told you that?" - trace negative self-talk to its origin (teacher, family member, etc.)
- Replace "should" with "want to" or "get to"
- Separate self-worth from outcomes, accomplishments, and productivity

**Stage 5 - Seeking instant gratification:**
- Identify your go-to comfort behaviors (scrolling, snacking, gaming)
- Evaluate: can you bounce back, or are these time-sucking?
- Alternatives: 10-minute walk, filling/nutritious snack, stretching

**Stage 6 - End-of-day regret:**
- Shame vs. Guilt distinction: Shame = "I am a failure" / Guilt = "I missed the mark today"
- Give yourself compassion and patience
- Explore: "What is my definition of success? Does this work for me?"

## Dopamenu

Framework for intentionally managing dopamine through categorized activities. Addresses the dopamine deficit in ADHD (Volkow et al., 2011) by making stimulation-seeking deliberate rather than reactive.

### Categories

**Entrees** - Activities that excite and energize (require more time/commitment):
- Intentional exercise (yoga, running, pickleball)
- Quality time with partner or friend
- Reading
- Developing a new hobby
- Gardening
- Trying a new recipe

**Starters** - Quick energy without much time commitment:
- Playing with / cuddling pets
- Stretching or no-prep movement
- 10-minute walk
- Cup of coffee or tea
- Timed tidy-up (5-minute timer to organize workspace)
- Healthy, crunchy snack

**Sides** - Add to other activities to increase engagement:
- Playlist / music
- Fidgets / movement
- Personal challenge (set smaller goals within the task)
- Body-doubling
- Scented candle

**Desserts** - Go-tos that tend to be overdone (use with awareness):
- Scrolling social media
- Mindless snacking
- Watching TV
- Video games

### Key Principles
- Dopamine is the chemical messenger supporting executive functioning
- Released when we experience pleasure, reinforcing that pleasure
- ADHD brains have consistently lower dopamine levels (Volkow et al., 2011)
- Personal interest drives dopamine more than obligation ("should")
- It's OK to reach for dopamine - the goal is intentional, not restrictive

## HALT Check-In

Recovery skill for moments of struggle. When struggling to stay on task, pause and check:

- **H**ungry?
- **A**ngry?
- **L**onely?
- **T**ired?

Use non-judgmentally. The point is identifying an unmet need, not adding another thing to feel bad about.

## Eisenhower Matrix

Prioritization tool mapping tasks on two axes: urgency and importance.

| | Urgent | Less Urgent |
|---|--------|-------------|
| **Important** | Do first | Delay (schedule it) |
| **Less Important** | Delegate | Delete |

Example from curriculum:
- Do first: Complete work assignment (due today)
- Delay: Drop package for return at post office (by next Tuesday)
- Delegate: Take trash to the road (before bed tonight)
- Delete: Mow the lawn

## SMART Goals

Framework for converting vague intentions into actionable goals:

- **S**pecific
- **M**easurable
- **A**chievable
- **R**ealistic
- **T**ime-Sensitive

## Focus as a Spectrum

Focus is not binary. The curriculum frames it as a spectrum with three zones:

**Distraction** (too little focus):
- Difficulty blocking external stimuli
- Stimulus overload - chronic difficulty tuning out distracting stimuli (Brown, 2008)
- "Messytasking" - attempting two things at once and making a mess of both (Crowell, 2022)

**Focus** (balanced):
- Balancing and shifting attention to stimuli
- Prioritizing what's important, ignoring what's not

**Hyperfocus** (intense, engrossed focus):
- Not inherently bad - can be helpful if directed at difficult tasks
- Problematic when directed at unproductive activities (forget obligations, lose track of time)

## Activation Skills

### The 600-Second Commitment
10-minute daily planning meeting with yourself. Intentionally plan the day using Eisenhower Matrix and SMART Goals.

### Organizing Space & Stuff
- "Don't put it down, put it away"
- "Subtract before you add" (donate before purchasing)
- Organize with a plan (bin method, Marie Kondo method)

### Making Tasks Activatable
- Make it new / reimagine it / change perspective
- Make it personally interesting / make it a game / incentivize it
- Make it urgent / schedule progress meetings / create accountability by telling someone

### Procrastivity
The act of choosing the less important task to continue procrastinating the more daunting one (Ramsay, 2023). Recognizing this pattern is the first step.

## Effort & Sleep

### Sleep-ADHD Connection
- Biological: neurotransmitters implicated in ADHD are also involved in sleep
- Genetic: COMT gene linked to both ADHD and sleep-wake regulation
- Symptomatic: poor time management, difficulty with organization, and trouble sustaining attention make it harder to shut down for sleep
- Revenge bedtime procrastination: late-night leisure time stolen from sleep

### Sleep Hygiene Skills
- Establish a set bedtime routine
- Build a restful environment (cool, dark, quiet)
- Phone out of arm's reach
- Bedroom boundaries: bed is only for sleep or intimacy
- Intentional wind-down before bed (don't use bedtime as wind-down time)
- Daytime = productive time, nighttime is not

### Sustaining Effort
- Dopamine deficit makes it harder to sustain effort without immediate reward
- Reduce distractions, time-limited breaks, use the Dopamenu
- Break tasks up (Goblin.tools)
- Mono-tasking over multi-tasking
- Body-doubling: working alongside another person for motivation (Barkley, 2023)

## Emotional Dysregulation

### What It Looks Like
- Difficulty managing frustration, anger, disappointment, desire, worry
- Wanting something urgently and having difficulty tolerating "not yet"
- Flooding: experiencing emotions as intense, overwhelming, immediate

### Emotional Hyperarousal
Especially passionate internal thoughts, feelings, and reactions associated with ADHD. Can show up as:
- Flash emotions
- Alexithymia / dyslexithymia (difficulty identifying or describing emotions)
- Lack of emotional awareness
- Overreaction
- Low frustration tolerance
- Rejection sensitivity
- Shame and guilt

### Two Ways Emotions Impact ADHD (Brown, 2017)
1. Executive functioning deficits impair ability to regulate emotional expression
2. Emotions influence motivations in decision-making and working memory, leading to problematic behaviors

### Emotion Regulation Skills

**Externalize It:**
- Journal about it (avoid rumination by getting thoughts out)
- Phone a friend (share a vulnerable feeling; clarify if you don't want feedback)
- Use the feelings wheel for daily emotional check-ins

**Ride the Wave:**
- Move through it - remind yourself feelings are temporary, like riding a wave
- Validate your experience - "It is ok that you feel this way. You will not always feel this way."
- Emotions are data - get curious about what they're trying to show you

**Practice Self-Compassion:**
- "No one can give you the love and kindness that YOU can give you"
- Mindful curiosity - explore feelings with curiosity instead of judgment

### Shame vs. Guilt
- Shame: "I never spend my time productively and I am a failure" (identity-level)
- Guilt: "I missed the mark today by spending my time doing this instead of that" (behavior-level)

## Working Memory

### What It Is
Holds one thing in mind while attending to another. Barkley describes it as "holding mental representations in mind that are pertinent to the tasks we have to do and govern our behavior toward our goals."

### Components
- **Verbal working memory**: words or self-talk (stronger in ADHD brains - 2x visual)
- **Visual working memory**: relevant images from past experience (weaker in ADHD brains)

### Common Manifestations
- Reading a page and not remembering what you read
- Forgetting appointment times
- Losing your thought while waiting for your turn to speak
- Walking into a room and forgetting why

### Skills
- Develop a routine (reduces working memory load)
- Daily exercise (research-backed correlation with improved working memory - Bailey, 2022)
- Daily mindfulness (correlated with improved recall - Bailey, 2022)
- External cues: checklists, calendars, reminders
- Lean on existing schedule/structure
- Memory devices: visualization, rhymes, songs
